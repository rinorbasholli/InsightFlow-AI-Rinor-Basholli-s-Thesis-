\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{cha:eval}

This chapter evaluates the laboratory. Section~\ref{sec:eval-strategy} describes the strategy and the provenance of the numbers. The following sections report the automated tests, the configured teaching values, the phishing benchmark, the adversarial evaluation, the recorded live calls and the verification of the requirements.

\section{Evaluation strategy}
\label{sec:eval-strategy}

The evaluation distinguishes three kinds of output, following the provenance contract of the laboratory:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Configured values}, written into the teaching material, such as the score bands of the seeded email set. They are reported as configuration and are not interpreted as findings.
\item \textbf{Computed results} on a constructed corpus: the phishing benchmark.
\item \textbf{Computed results} on a real pretrained model and a public dataset: the adversarial evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

Every number in this chapter is read from a file in the repository. The adversarial results were produced by commit \texttt{742a669}, which is recorded in the result file. The phishing benchmark regenerates its corpus and results from code; the corpus, the hold-out predictions and the random-fold cross-validation were reproduced identically in a second environment. That check revealed an indentation error, introduced in a later commit, that made the script regenerate only part of the corpus; the error was corrected, and the corrected script reproduces the published corpus and results exactly. The phishing result file records the Python and scikit-learn versions and the Git commit of the run.

\section{Automated tests}
\label{sec:eval-tests}

The test suite described in Section~\ref{sec:tests} was run with \texttt{npm test}. All 34 tests passed (28 security-function tests and 6 contract tests) in less than one second, without network access. The tests verify the security requirements NFR1--NFR4 and NFR11 at the level of functions, and the provenance contract FR9 statically, by inspecting the source code; they do not exercise the routes through HTTP.

\section{Configured teaching values}
\label{sec:eval-config}

Loading the seeded teaching set produces 90 emails (30 per class) and the dashboard aggregates shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ui-dash}: a mean designed realism of 75.12 (displayed as 75\%), a designed AI-class gap of 51.23 (100 minus the mean detectability of the AI-style class, displayed as 51\%) and a mean designed detectability of 53.67. These values follow from the bands in Table~\ref{tab:bands}, from the fixed scores of the three exemplars and from the fixed seed. They illustrate the intended contrast between the classes; they are not measurements of a mail filter or of human performance, and the dashboard labels them as designed values.

\section{Phishing benchmark}
\label{sec:eval-phish}

\subsection{Corpus and protocol}

The benchmark corpus contains 187 emails constructed for this work around the email patterns of a Portuguese polytechnic institute, in English and Portuguese (Table~\ref{tab:corpus}); Appendix~\ref{app:ai} states how the corpus was authored. It is separate from the seeded teaching set of the dashboard, and the two use different class names, mapped in Table~\ref{tab:classes}. Legitimate (\emph{ham}) and phishing messages deliberately share subjects, urgency, institutional vocabulary and occasional typing errors: 44 of the 48 distinct subjects occur in both classes. The phishing class contains conventional lures and look-alike spear-phishing messages written in the polished, context-specific style associated with LLM-generated phishing. The corpus does not contain output of the laboratory's own generator, so it does not measure the detection of that output.

Three models were compared (Section~\ref{sec:impl-eval}): \emph{wording only} (subject and body), \emph{with sender} (subject, body and \texttt{From} address) and an always-phishing \emph{baseline}. Phishing is the positive class. Each model was evaluated in three ways: on a stratified 70/30 hold-out split; with stratified five-fold cross-validation over random folds; and with stratified five-fold cross-validation grouped by sender domain, in which no domain appears in both the training and the test fold. The grouped variant is needed because the 187 emails come from 33 sender domains, 23 of which occur more than once, so a model evaluated on random folds can recognise domains it has already seen. The wording-only model still sees link host names that appear in message bodies, so it is not completely free of domain information.

\subsection{Interpretation}

On this corpus, the wording of a message carries little information about its class. Under every protocol, the wording-only model has an F1 no better than the always-phishing baseline (0.708 against 0.736 on random folds, 0.699 against 0.734 on unseen domains), and its AUC of 0.60--0.63 indicates weak ranking ability; on the hold-out split it recognises only 2 of 24 legitimate emails.

The sender address helps, but less than the random-fold results suggest. On random folds, adding the sender raises F1 to 0.806 and AUC to 0.837. When the test folds contain only sender domains that were not seen in training, F1 falls to 0.743, practically equal to the baseline, while AUC remains clearly higher than for wording alone (0.784 against 0.602). Two conclusions follow. First, part of the random-fold advantage comes from memorising specific domains. Second, the form of an unseen domain still helps to \emph{rank} messages, but at the default decision threshold the classifier labels almost every message as phishing; with the sender, it still misclassifies 18 of the 24 legitimate emails of the hold-out split.

The weak wording signal is largely a property of how the corpus was built: legitimate and phishing messages share subjects and tone, and a number of bodies in both classes contain explanatory phrases about phishing cues. The result is therefore not evidence about real mail streams. It is consistent with earlier detectors that relied mainly on structural and sender-related features rather than wording~\cite{fette2007learning}, and with the defensive emphasis of the laboratory on authenticating the sending domain with SPF, DKIM and DMARC~\cite{rfc7208,rfc6376,rfc9989}, which does not depend on recognising either the wording or the specific domain.

Twenty emails were selected across the four pattern families (\path{MANUAL_REVIEW.csv}) and their labels were re-checked manually; all twenty were confirmed. This step detects clerical labelling errors but, because I carried it out myself, it is not an independent validation of the labels.

\section{Adversarial evaluation}
\label{sec:eval-adv}

\subsection{Protocol}

The evaluation follows the principles of Carlini et al.~\cite{carlini2019evaluating} where they apply to an undefended model: the threat model is stated explicitly (untargeted, white-box, $L_\infty$-bounded perturbations in pixel space), an iterative attack is used in addition to a single-step attack, and the perturbation bound is verified on every example. Two hundred images were drawn from the CIFAR-10 test set with seed 20260927. Clean accuracy was measured, and FGSM and PGD (10 steps, step size $\varepsilon/4$, no random start) were run at $\varepsilon \in \{2/255, 8/255, 16/255\}$ against the pretrained ResNet-56. Robust accuracy is the share of all 200 images still classified correctly after the attack, reported with a Wilson 95\% confidence interval. The attack success rate (ASR) is the share of the images classified correctly \emph{before} the attack whose prediction becomes wrong after it. The flip rate is the share of all 200 images whose prediction changes.

\subsection{Results}

The model classified 191 of the 200 images correctly before any attack (clean accuracy 0.955). Table~\ref{tab:adv} reports the results. The perturbation size equals the budget: for both attacks, the mean and the maximum measured $L_\infty$ were 0.00784, 0.03137 and 0.06275 at the three budgets, equal to $2/255$, $8/255$ and $16/255$ up to floating-point precision, so every adversarial example respected its budget (NFR6).

\subsection{Interpretation}

The undefended model is highly vulnerable. Already at $\varepsilon = 2/255$, a change of at most two intensity levels per 8-bit colour channel, FGSM reduces accuracy from 0.955 to 0.45 and PGD to 0.05. At $8/255$, the budget commonly used in the robustness literature~\cite{madry2018towards}, PGD misclassifies every image that was correctly classified before the attack. PGD is consistently stronger than FGSM, as expected from an iterative attack, and robust accuracy decreases monotonically with the budget. These observations agree with the sanity checks recommended by Carlini et al.~\cite{carlini2019evaluating}: accuracy reaches zero for large budgets, and the iterative attack dominates the single-step attack. Because PGD was run without random restarts, the PGD figures are an upper bound on robust accuracy; at $8/255$ and $16/255$ they are already zero. The results apply to this model and this sample of 200 images; the confidence intervals in Table~\ref{tab:adv} quantify the sampling uncertainty.

\section{Recorded live calls}
\label{sec:eval-live}

The live attack service was called three times through its API on the first correctly classified \emph{airplane} test image (index 3), which the model classifies as airplane with a confidence that rounds to 100.00\%. With $\varepsilon = 0.05$, untargeted FGSM changed the prediction to \emph{frog} (79.94\%), untargeted PGD to \emph{ship} (100.00\% after rounding), and targeted FGSM towards \emph{bird} reached its target (99.98\%). In all three cases the measured $L_\infty$ was 0.05000. These single-image results demonstrate the live module and the budget check; they are not statistics and are not combined with Table~\ref{tab:adv}.

The three language routes were each recorded once with the Gemini model \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash} (\path{eval/gemini_runs/}). All three recorded responses carry \texttt{source: gemini} and HTTP~200, and the analysis and deepfake calls took 9.67\,s and 9.26\,s respectively. The first generation attempt met a temporary HTTP~503 from the API; the server answered it with a labelled fallback, which was not kept, and the recorded response is the second attempt. The summary file counts only the kept responses. The recorded email analysis classified its input, a scholarship-payment lure sent from a non-institutional domain, as \texttt{conventional\_phishing}; the input itself was not stored with the response. These recordings show that the routes work with the live model; the accuracy of the LLM-based email analysis was not measured (Section~\ref{sec:limits-disc}). The recordings predate the introduction of the untrusted-data markers in the analysis prompt (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Summary}

The security controls pass their automated tests. The adversarial evaluation shows that an undefended CIFAR-10 classifier loses almost all accuracy under standard $L_\infty$ budgets. The phishing benchmark shows that, on a corpus in which legitimate and phishing messages share their wording, wording alone does not beat a trivial baseline; the sender helps to rank messages even for unseen domains, but most of its apparent F1 advantage depends on having seen the domain before. Chapter~\ref{cha:disc} discusses what these results mean and where they stop.
