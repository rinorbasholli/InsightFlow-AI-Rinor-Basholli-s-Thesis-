\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{cha:eval}

This chapter evaluates the laboratory. Section~\ref{sec:eval-strategy} describes the strategy and the provenance of the numbers. The following sections report the automated tests, the configured teaching values, the phishing benchmark, the adversarial evaluation, the recorded live calls and the verification of the requirements.

\section{Evaluation strategy}
\label{sec:eval-strategy}

The evaluation distinguishes three kinds of output, following the provenance contract of the laboratory:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Configured values}, written by the author into the teaching material, such as the score bands of the seeded email set. They are reported as configuration and are not interpreted as findings.
\item \textbf{Computed results} on a reconstructed corpus: the phishing benchmark.
\item \textbf{Computed results} on a real pretrained model and a public dataset: the adversarial evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

Every number in this chapter is read from a file in the repository. The adversarial results were produced by commit \texttt{742a669} (recorded in the result file); the phishing results are regenerated identically by the benchmark script and were reproduced independently in a clean environment, yielding byte-identical metric and prediction files.

\section{Automated tests}
\label{sec:eval-tests}

The test suite described in Section~\ref{sec:tests} was run with \texttt{npm test}. All 31 tests passed (25 security-function tests and 6 contract tests) in less than one second, without network access. The tests verify the security requirements NFR1--NFR4 and the provenance contract FR9 at the level of functions and source code; they do not exercise the routes through HTTP.

\section{Configured teaching values}
\label{sec:eval-config}

Loading the seeded teaching set produces 90 emails (30 per class) and the dashboard aggregates shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ui-dash}: a mean designed realism of 75.12 (displayed as 75\%), a designed AI-class gap of 51.23 (100 minus the mean detectability of the AI-style class, displayed as 51\%) and a mean designed detectability of 53.67. They illustrate the intended contrast between the classes; they are not measurements of a mail filter or of human performance, and the dashboard labels them as designed values.

\section{Phishing benchmark}
\label{sec:eval-phish}

\subsection{Corpus and protocol}

The benchmark corpus contains 187 emails written for this work around the email patterns of a Portuguese polytechnic institute, in English and Portuguese (Table~\ref{tab:corpus}). Legitimate (\emph{ham}) and phishing messages deliberately share subjects, urgency, institutional vocabulary and occasional typing errors, so that the classes cannot be separated by superficial wording. The phishing class contains conventional lures and look-alike spear-phishing messages in the polished style that LLMs produce; these messages were written by the author, not generated by an LLM. All labels were assigned by the author.

Three models were compared (Section~\ref{sec:impl-eval}): \emph{wording only} (subject and body), \emph{with sender} (subject, body and \texttt{From} address) and an always-phishing \emph{baseline}. \subsection{Interpretation}

On this corpus, the wording of a message carries little information about its class. The wording-only model does not outperform the trivial baseline in F1 (0.708 against 0.736 under cross-validation), and its AUC of 0.629 indicates weak ranking ability; on the hold-out split its AUC of 0.513 is at chance level and it recognises only 2 of 24 legitimate emails. Adding the sender address raises cross-validated F1 to 0.806 and AUC to 0.837, clearly above the baseline. The discriminative signal therefore lies mainly in the sender domain.

This outcome is a consequence of how the corpus was built: when legitimate and phishing messages share vocabulary and tone, as LLM-written phishing does in practice~\cite{hazell2023spear,heiding2024devising}, a bag-of-n-grams model cannot separate them by wording. The result is consistent with earlier work in which structural and sender-related features carried most of the signal~\cite{fette2007learning}, and it supports the defensive emphasis of the laboratory on verifying the sending domain, for example with SPF, DKIM and DMARC~\cite{rfc7208,rfc6376,rfc9989}. The numbers describe this reconstructed corpus only; they are not estimates of the performance of any mail filter.

\subsection{Label check}

Twenty emails were selected across the four pattern families (\path{MANUAL_REVIEW.csv}) and their labels were re-checked manually; all twenty were confirmed. Because the same person wrote, labelled and re-checked the emails, this step detects clerical labelling errors but does not provide independent validation of the labels.

\section{Adversarial evaluation}
\label{sec:eval-adv}

\subsection{Protocol}

The evaluation follows the principles of Carlini et al.~\cite{carlini2019evaluating} where they apply to an undefended model: the threat model is stated explicitly (untargeted, white-box, $L_\infty$-bounded perturbations in pixel space), an iterative attack is used in addition to a single-step attack, and the perturbation bound is verified on every example. Two hundred images were drawn from the CIFAR-10 test set with seed 20260927. Clean accuracy was measured, and FGSM and PGD (10 steps, step size $\varepsilon/4$, no random start) were run at $\varepsilon \in \{2/255, 8/255, 16/255\}$ against the pretrained ResNet-56. Robust accuracy is the share of all 200 images still classified correctly after the attack, reported with a Wilson 95\% confidence interval. The attack success rate (ASR) is the share of the images classified correctly \emph{before} the attack whose prediction becomes wrong after it. The flip rate is the share of all 200 images whose prediction changes.

\subsection{Results}

The model classified 191 of the 200 images correctly before any attack (clean accuracy 0.955). Table~\ref{tab:adv} reports the results. In every bucket the maximum measured $L_\infty$ was equal to the budget up to floating-point precision, so all adversarial examples respected their budget (NFR6).

\subsection{Interpretation}

The undefended model is highly vulnerable. Already at $\varepsilon = 2/255$, a change of at most two intensity levels per 8-bit colour channel, FGSM reduces accuracy from 0.955 to 0.45 and PGD to 0.05. At $8/255$, the budget commonly used in the robustness literature~\cite{madry2018towards}, PGD misclassifies every image that was correctly classified before the attack. PGD is consistently stronger than FGSM, as expected from an iterative attack, and robust accuracy decreases monotonically with the budget. These observations agree with the sanity checks recommended by Carlini et al.~\cite{carlini2019evaluating}: accuracy reaches zero for large budgets, and the iterative attack dominates the single-step attack. Because PGD was run without random restarts, the PGD figures are an upper bound on robust accuracy; at $8/255$ and $16/255$ they are already zero. The results apply to this model and this sample of 200 images; the confidence intervals in Table~\ref{tab:adv} quantify the sampling uncertainty.

\section{Recorded live calls}
\label{sec:eval-live}

The live attack service was called three times on the first correctly classified \emph{airplane} test image (index 3), which the model classifies as airplane with 100\% confidence. With $\varepsilon = 0.05$, untargeted FGSM changed the prediction to \emph{frog} (79.94\%), untargeted PGD to \emph{ship} (100\%), and targeted FGSM towards \emph{bird} reached its target (99.98\%). In all three cases the measured $L_\infty$ was 0.05000. These single-image results demonstrate the live module and the budget check; they are not statistics and are not combined with Table~\ref{tab:adv}.

The three language routes were each recorded once with the Gemini model \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash} (\path{eval/gemini_runs/}). All three returned \texttt{source: gemini} with HTTP~200; the generation call succeeded on its second attempt after a temporary HTTP~503 from the API, and the analysis and deepfake calls took 9.67\,s and 9.26\,s respectively. The recorded email analysis classified a scholarship-payment lure from a non-institutional domain as \texttt{conventional\_phishing}. These recordings show that the routes work with the live model; the accuracy of the LLM-based email analysis was not measured (Section~\ref{sec:limits-disc}). The recordings predate the introduction of the untrusted-data markers in the analysis prompt (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Summary}

The security controls pass their automated tests, the adversarial evaluation shows that an undefended CIFAR-10 classifier loses almost all accuracy under standard $L_\infty$ budgets, and the phishing benchmark shows that, on a corpus in which legitimate and phishing messages share their wording, the sender carries the discriminative signal. Chapter~\ref{cha:disc} discusses what these results mean and where they stop.
