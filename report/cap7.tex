\chapter{Discussion}
\label{cha:disc}

This chapter interprets the results in relation to the literature, examines the threats to their validity, lists the limitations of the laboratory, discusses ethics and dual use, and presents the STRIDE analysis of the system.

\section{Interpretation of the results}
\label{sec:interp}

\paragraph{Phishing.} On the constructed corpus, wording alone does not separate phishing from legitimate mail, and the sender helps mainly to rank messages: its F1 advantage largely disappears when the test domains have not been seen in training. The first observation is consistent with the concern raised by work on LLM-generated phishing, namely that polished, tailored text no longer distinguishes attacks~\cite{hazell2023spear,heiding2024devising}; the corpus was built to reproduce that situation, so the result illustrates the concern rather than confirming it for real mail. The second observation is consistent with earlier detectors that relied on structural and sender-related features~\cite{fette2007learning}, and it shows the limit of learning from domain names: a classifier can memorise known malicious domains but does not reliably recognise new ones. The practical implication for teaching is to shift attention from spelling and tone towards verifying the sender and the destination of links, and, for organisations, towards domain authentication~\cite{rfc9989}, which does not depend on recognising either.

\paragraph{Adversarial examples.} The near-complete loss of accuracy of an undefended ResNet-56 under PGD at $8/255$ reproduces, on a small sample, the well-known fragility of standard training~\cite{szegedy2014intriguing,madry2018towards}. The contribution of the module is not this finding, which is expected, but its presentation: learners obtain real model outputs, with the budget verified, in the same environment in which they meet LLM-generated content, and they can see that a confident softmax output is not evidence of correctness.

\paragraph{Deepfakes.} The scenario module answers RQ2 only at the level of design. It shows that awareness content can be delivered without processing media and with explicit provenance, and that the defences it teaches (out-of-band verification, provenance checks) do not depend on detecting artefacts. It cannot show that learners become better at recognising deepfakes; that would require a study with participants.

\paragraph{Provenance.} Across the three modules, the \texttt{source} label separates computed results from LLM narration, presets and fallbacks. The server code is checked statically by a contract test, and the client displays the label it receives, treating a missing or unknown label as a fallback and refusing to show adversarial values without \texttt{source: computed}. This is the central design property of the laboratory; it is enforced by these checks rather than by convention alone, but it is not verified end to end through HTTP.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:validity}

\paragraph{Construct validity.} The phishing family labelled ``look-alike / spear-phishing'' imitates the style of LLM-generated phishing but does not contain output of the laboratory's generator, so the benchmark does not measure the detection of LLM-generated email. The wording-only model is not strictly content-only, because message bodies contain link host names. The configured dashboard values do not measure any construct and are excluded from the findings.

\paragraph{Internal validity.} The corpus was constructed and labelled in one process, and the manual check of twenty labels was not independent, so the classifiers may learn regularities of the construction rather than of phishing. Two such regularities are visible: 44 of the 48 subjects occur in both classes, which removes the subject as a signal by design, and a number of message bodies in both classes contain explanatory phrases about phishing cues. Sender domains repeat across the corpus, which inflates random-fold results; the grouped cross-validation in Table~\ref{tab:phish-cv} controls for this. The PGD attack uses ten steps without random restarts, which can overestimate robust accuracy; at $8/255$ and above this does not affect the conclusion, because robust accuracy is already zero.

\paragraph{External validity.} The phishing results apply to a small constructed corpus of 187 emails from one institutional context and do not transfer to real mail streams. The adversarial results apply to one pretrained model on 200 CIFAR-10 test images; other architectures, datasets or defended models may behave differently.

\paragraph{Reliability.} Both evaluations use fixed seeds and regenerate their results from code, and the result files record the commit and library versions. The dependencies in \path{eval/requirements.txt} specify minimum versions or none at all rather than exact versions, and the model weights are loaded from the default branch of their repository through \texttt{torch.hub}, so future upstream changes could alter the results. The CIFAR-10 test set must be downloaded once before the batch script runs (Appendix~\ref{app:eval}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits-disc}

Beyond the threats above, the laboratory has the following limitations.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The LLM-based email analysis is not evaluated.} The classification that learners see in the analysis module comes from Gemini, whose accuracy on the benchmark corpus was not measured; the TF-IDF benchmark evaluates a different model. Published results for LLM detectors~\cite{koide2024chatspamdetector} do not transfer to this corpus.
\item \textbf{No learner study.} The educational effect of the laboratory has not been measured. Prior work shows how such an evaluation can be designed~\cite{sheng2007phil}.
\item \textbf{No media analysis.} The deepfake module does not inspect files and cannot demonstrate detection.
\item \textbf{Residual prompt-injection risk.} The denylist covers only naive direct-injection phrasing~\cite{perez2022ignore}, and the untrusted-data markers in the analysis prompt reduce but do not remove the risk of indirect injection~\cite{greshake2023signed}. The model is asked for structured output, but the server only parses it and normalises the class name; it does not validate the other values against the schema.
\item \textbf{Deployment-dependent client address.} Rate limiting relies on the client address. With the default configuration (\texttt{TRUST\_PROXY=0}) the address is the socket peer and cannot be forged; behind a reverse proxy, \texttt{TRUST\_PROXY} must equal the number of trusted proxies, otherwise either all clients share one limit or a client can choose its address.
\item \textbf{Single-instance state.} Sessions, rate-limit counters and failed-sign-in counters are held in process memory; they are lost on restart and are not shared between instances (Section~\ref{sec:deploy}).
\item \textbf{Shared credentials and no audit log.} All users share one credential pair, and requests are not logged per user.
\item \textbf{Tests at function level.} The automated tests do not exercise the HTTP routes end to end, and the fallback and 503 paths are verified by inspection rather than by tests.
\end{itemize}

\section{Ethics and dual use}
\label{sec:ethics}

\paragraph{Dual use.} A phishing generator can serve attackers as well as learners, particularly when it produces lures tailored to a specific institution. The laboratory reduces this risk on the access side: it requires authentication and fails closed in production, limits failed sign-in attempts, limits generation to ten requests per hour per client, rejects obvious injection attempts, and has no function for sending email. These measures restrict \emph{who} can generate content and \emph{how much}, but not \emph{what} is generated. Output-side controls, such as a visible marker inserted into every generated message or replacing links with non-resolvable placeholders, were not implemented and are proposed as future work. The adversarial module attacks a public benchmark model, not a deployed system, and the deepfake module produces no synthetic media.

\paragraph{Data protection.} The analysis module accepts pasted text and \texttt{.eml} files, which may contain personal data of third parties, and forwards the extracted text to an external LLM provider. The laboratory stores none of this content on its server, but the transfer itself is a processing of personal data within the meaning of the General Data Protection Regulation~\cite{gdpr2016}, and whether the provider may retain or use submitted content depends on the terms of the API service in use, which the operator must check. In its current form the module should therefore be used with the teaching material supplied with the laboratory or with messages from which personal data has been removed, and users should be told so on the analysis screen. This notice is not yet shown in the interface.

\paragraph{Honesty of outputs.} Presenting LLM-written scores as measurements would mislead learners about what AI systems can establish. The provenance labels, the absence of a fallback on the adversarial route and the labelling of configured dashboard values address this risk directly.

\section{Summary}

The results support three claims within their scope: on the constructed corpus, wording does not separate the classes and the sender helps to rank messages, mostly by recognising known domains; an undefended CIFAR-10 classifier is fragile under standard $L_\infty$ budgets; and the provenance of every output can be made explicit and checked. They do not support claims about real mail streams, about deepfake detection or about learning outcomes. Chapter~\ref{cha:conc} draws the conclusions.
