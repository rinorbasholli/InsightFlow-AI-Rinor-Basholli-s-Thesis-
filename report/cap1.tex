\chapter{Introduction}
\label{cha:intro}

This chapter introduces the context and motivation of the work, states the problem it addresses, and defines the aim, objectives and research questions. It then delimits the scope, outlines the methodology, lists the contributions and describes the structure of the report.

\section{Context and motivation}
\label{sec:context}

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) has lowered the cost of three classes of attack that security courses have traditionally treated separately. Large language models (LLMs) can produce fluent, context-specific phishing messages at scale~\cite{hazell2023spear,heiding2024devising}. Generative image and audio models can produce faces and voices of people who never recorded them~\cite{karras2019stylegan,mirsky2021deepfake}. Image classifiers can be made to change their prediction through perturbations that are small in pixel space~\cite{szegedy2014intriguing,goodfellow2015explaining}. Phishing in particular remains one of the most frequent initial-access vectors reported by industry and public bodies~\cite{enisa2024threat,apwg2024}.

For students of informatics engineering, these risks are usually encountered through separate artefacts: a phishing-campaign tool such as GoPhish~\cite{gophish}, research datasets for deepfake detection~\cite{rossler2019faceforensics,li2020celebdf}, and adversarial-attack libraries or notebooks~\cite{kim2020torchattacks}. Each artefact is valuable on its own, but none of them shows the three risks side by side, and none makes explicit whether a displayed score was measured by a model, generated by an LLM, or written into the tool by its author. That distinction matters in a security course, because a student who cannot tell a measurement from a narrated estimate learns the wrong lesson about AI outputs.

\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Two problems motivate this work. The first is fragmentation: there is no single, small, self-hostable laboratory in which a student can generate and inspect AI-assisted phishing, examine deepfake scenarios, and run real adversarial attacks within one workflow. The second is provenance: educational tools that call LLMs can easily present model-generated numbers, such as a ``realism score'' or a ``confidence'', as if they were measurements. A laboratory intended to teach AI security risks must therefore separate computed results from model narration and from configured teaching values, and it must protect its own LLM-backed functions against misuse.

\section{Aim and objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The aim of this work is to design, implement and technically evaluate a web laboratory that demonstrates AI-generated phishing, deepfake scenarios and adversarial examples in one application, while labelling the provenance of every analytical output. The aim is broken down into five verifiable objectives:

\begin{description}
\item[O1] Implement one web application with three laboratory modules (phishing, deepfake scenarios, adversarial attacks) and a dashboard.
\item[O2] Label every successful analytical response with its provenance (\texttt{gemini}, \texttt{fallback}, \texttt{preset} or \texttt{computed}), and never substitute LLM output for a computed adversarial result.
\item[O3] Compute adversarial results with real Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) and Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) attacks on a pretrained classifier over at least 200 test images, reporting clean accuracy, robust accuracy, attack success rate and perturbation size.
\item[O4] Provide a reproducible phishing-classification benchmark with accuracy, precision, recall, F1 and area under the ROC curve (AUC), compared against a trivial baseline.
\item[O5] Protect the deployed laboratory with authentication, rate limiting, request-size limits and prompt-injection mitigations, analyse it with STRIDE, and verify the controls with automated tests.
\end{description}

Chapter~\ref{cha:conc} revisits each objective and states whether it was met.

\section{Research questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

Three research questions guide the work. Each is given an evaluation criterion so that it can be answered from the evidence produced.

\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] Within a constructed corpus of institutional emails, how much does the message wording, as opposed to the sender address, contribute to separating phishing from legitimate mail? \emph{Criterion:} cross-validated F1 and AUC of a wording-only and a with-sender classifier, on random folds and on folds with unseen sender domains, compared with an always-phishing baseline.
\item[RQ2] How can a deepfake module support awareness teaching without processing media, and what are the limits of that design? \emph{Criterion:} a design that labels every output as preset, LLM narration or fallback, and an explicit statement of what such a design cannot show.
\item[RQ3] How vulnerable is an undefended, pretrained CIFAR-10 classifier to FGSM and PGD under common $L_\infty$ budgets? \emph{Criterion:} robust accuracy with 95\% confidence intervals and attack success rate at $\varepsilon \in \{2/255, 8/255, 16/255\}$, with every perturbation verified to satisfy $\|\delta\|_\infty \le \varepsilon$.
\end{description}

\section{Scope and delimitations}
\label{sec:scope}

The laboratory is an educational demonstrator, not a production detector. It does not send email, does not harvest mailboxes, and does not inspect uploaded media files: the deepfake module reasons over a filename, a media type and a textual description. The phishing benchmark uses a corpus constructed for this work, not real user mail, and its results describe that corpus only. The adversarial evaluation uses one pretrained model on CIFAR-10~\cite{krizhevsky2009cifar} and does not evaluate defences. No study with learners was carried out; the educational effect of the laboratory is therefore not measured in this report.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

The work follows an iterative engineering approach with a technical evaluation. Requirements were derived from the problem statement, from feedback received during development and from a STRIDE threat analysis~\cite{shostack2014threat}, and were refined as the implementation progressed. The resulting artefact was evaluated in three ways: (i) automated unit and contract tests of the security controls; (ii) a reproducible text-classification benchmark on a reconstructed corpus, reported with five-fold cross-validation and a trivial baseline; and (iii) a reproducible batch of adversarial attacks on 200 CIFAR-10 test images, reported with Wilson confidence intervals. Every number reported in Chapter~\ref{cha:eval} is read from a result file in the repository, together with the commit that produced it.

Related work was identified through targeted searches of peer-reviewed venues and preprint servers (ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, arXiv, proceedings of ICLR, ICML, SOUPS and Interspeech), official standards (IETF RFCs) and official technical documentation, using the terms \emph{LLM phishing}, \emph{phishing email detection}, \emph{deepfake detection}, \emph{adversarial robustness evaluation}, \emph{prompt injection} and \emph{anti-phishing training}. The searches and the checking of each source against its publisher or index page were carried out with the assistance of an AI tool, as declared in Appendix~\ref{app:ai}.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contrib}

The contributions of this work are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{InsightFlow AI}, a React and Express web laboratory with a separate Python attack service, which places AI-generated phishing, deepfake scenarios and adversarial attacks in one authenticated workflow (O1).
\item \textbf{A provenance contract}: every successful analytical response carries a \texttt{source} field, enforced by automated tests, and the adversarial route returns HTTP~503 rather than an invented value when its service is unavailable (O2).
\item \textbf{A reproducible adversarial evaluation} of a pretrained CIFAR-10 ResNet-56 under FGSM and PGD at three $L_\infty$ budgets, with confidence intervals and verified perturbation bounds, sharing its attack implementation with the live laboratory (O3).
\item \textbf{A reproducible phishing benchmark} that separates wording-only from with-sender features, compares both with a trivial baseline and controls for memorised sender domains, showing that on the constructed corpus wording alone does not beat the baseline and that the sender's advantage depends largely on known domains (O4).
\item \textbf{A security design for an LLM-backed teaching tool}: fail-closed authentication, rate and size limits, prompt-injection mitigations, a STRIDE analysis and 34 automated tests (O5).
\end{itemize}
